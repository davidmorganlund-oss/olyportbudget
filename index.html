The Port Budget in Plain Words
An independent explainer. Not affiliated with the Port of Olympia.
Draft numbers. This page uses the Port's 2027 preliminary budget, with figures as of Oct. 27, 2026. The Port Commission is scheduled to vote on the final budget Nov. 23, 2026.

The short version

  • The Port's businesses cover their running costs, and not much more. In the 2027 draft they fall about $3.5M short of covering wear and tear on docks, pavement and buildings.
  • You pay the Port about $63 a year on a $500,000 home. That is a little over 1 cent of each property tax dollar.
  • The Budd Inlet cleanup has no firm price yet. The Port's own estimates run from $100M to $200M. The 2027 draft sets aside $3M.
  • Old loans are nearly paid off. Yearly payments drop by about $3.3M starting in 2029, and the Commission will decide where that money goes.

Question 1

Does the Port pay its own way?

Day to day, just barely. For every dollar the Port's businesses earn, they spend a dollar running themselves. Nothing is left to replace docks, pavement and buildings as they wear out.

The businesses bring in
$16.4M
From customers and tenants at the airport, marina, marine terminal and downtown properties.
They cost to run
$16.4M
$11.9M in direct costs plus $4.5M in shared office overhead. Left over: about $37,000.
Wear and tear not covered
$3.5M
The Port's things lose about $3.6M in value this year. The businesses earn almost none of it back.

Property taxes and grants pay for the public work on top: environmental cleanup, loan payments, running the public agency and most new construction.

How the Port sees it: the Port uses a different test. It says a public port succeeds when its businesses cover their direct running costs, and that property taxes and grants are meant to pay for cleanup, loan payments and new construction. By that test all four businesses pass in 2027, with $4.57M left before overhead. The Port also says it budgeted cautiously for 2027 because it expects fewer cargo ships. This page counts overhead and wear and tear as well, because those costs still have to be paid.

Source: 2027 Preliminary Operating Budget, pages 8, 9 and 17.

Question 2

What do I pay, and where does it go?

Everyone who owns property in Thurston County pays a Port tax. The draft rate for 2027 is 12.5 cents for every $1,000 your home is assessed at.

$

Your assessed value is on your Thurston County property tax statement.

You pay the Port about
$63
a year
Paying off old loans$26
Environmental cleanup$23
Running the public agency$14

How the split works: Port policy says the tax pays for three things, which add up to about $10.07M in 2027. The tax brings in $8.30M. This page divides your bill in the same proportions. That split is this page's estimate, not a Port figure. The remaining $1.76M comes from business earnings and savings. A separate $81,000 added to recover tax refunds would raise the rate slightly, to 12.6 cents.

How the Port tax works

As the Thurston County Treasurer explained it to the Port Commission on Nov. 9, 2026.

The budget sets the tax, not your home's value

The Commission decides how much to collect in total. Your assessed value only decides your share of that total.

When values rise, the rate falls

For 2026 the Port collected $8.21M across $66.4 billion of property. That works out to about 12.4 cents per $1,000.

Growth is capped

The total can rise 1% a year, plus tax on new construction. Going higher takes a vote of the people.

The Port is a small slice of your bill

The average total property tax rate in Thurston County is about $9.83 per $1,000. By this page's estimate, the Port is a little over 1 cent of each property tax dollar. Your own total rate depends on where you live.

Source: 2027 Preliminary Operating Budget, pages 26, 27 and 40. How the tax works: Thurston County Treasurer, presentation to the Port Commission, Nov. 9, 2026.

Question 3

How is each business doing?

The Port runs four businesses. Each one gets the same three tests, from easiest to hardest.

Airport and industrial land

Brings in $3.78M, mostly from land leases in Tumwater

Covers its running costsYes, +$1.27M
Covers its share of overheadNo, −$30K
Covers wear and tearNo, −$1.01M

This is a change. The airport and its industrial land were budgeted to come out ahead after wear and tear every year from 2017 through 2022. Since 2023 they have been budgeted short. Their share of overhead rose from $0.77M in 2020 to $1.30M in 2027.

A note on the middle test: the Port divides its office overhead by fixed shares (28% terminal, 26% airport, 26% marina, 10% downtown, 10% environmental). A different split would change which businesses pass.

Question 4

What about the cleanup?

The Port is responsible for cleaning up pollution in Budd Inlet and at the old Cascade Pole wood-treating site. Here is what the 2027 draft sets aside.

Budd Inlet, this year
$3.0M
For engineering design. The Port expects $2.0M of it back from grants. Last year's budget was $4.0M.
Cascade Pole, this year
$1.19M

There is no firm total yet. In 2024 the Port said early estimates were over $100M and could reach about $200M, plus $22M for testing and design. It had lined up $12M at that time.

In fairness

What is going well?

Several things in the 2027 draft point in a good direction.

Debt is going down

The Port plans no new borrowing. What it owes falls from $14.6M to $7.5M by 2031.

Every business covers its running costs

All four bring in more than they spend on direct operations.

Grants stretch local dollars

The Port expects grants to pay $8.3M of its $16.2M in 2027 construction. Most are not yet awarded.

The tax rate is low for a port

In 2025 it ranked 27th highest of 32 Washington counties with a port tax.

Source: 2027 Preliminary Operating Budget, pages 17, 20, 25 and 26.

How to read this: each row is that year's plan as the Commission adopted it, not what actually happened. Results were sometimes better. The Port's later budget books report that the businesses finished ahead after wear and tear in 2019, 2022 and 2023, and short in the other seven years from 2016 through 2025. In 2022 and 2023, the gain came from interest earned on savings ($2.45M and $2.99M), which those reports counted as business income. Without it, both years were short. Question 8

Why is this so hard to follow?

Because the Port's budget gives several answers to the same question, and the categories change from year to year. Here are four examples from the Port's own documents.

One year, five bottom lines

The 2027 draft reports all of these for the same year:

Operating surplus+$4.57M
Net margin+$37K
Operations deficit−$3.54M
Total net income+$7.18M
Change in savings−$8.92M

Each one is accurate. Which one you read first decides whether the year looks good or bad.

Money moves between categories

The Port's results for 2022, 2023 and 2025 counted interest earned on savings as income of the businesses: $2.45M, $2.99M and about $2.85M. In other years the same kind of income is counted separately. That makes those three years look stronger than the rest. The Port notes this for 2025 in its budget book.

Last year's numbers get rewritten

The 2026 budget, as adopted, showed the businesses $3.32M short after wear and tear. The 2027 book lists that same 2026 budget as $2.66M short. The 2020 budget showed $0.29M left before wear and tear. A year later, the 2021 book listed the 2020 budget at $1.95M.

The tax figure changed after the vote

The Port calculated its 2026 property tax at $8.33M. Thurston County corrected the calculation, and the amount collected is $8.21M.

What this means: none of these examples shows that a number is wrong, and regrouping costs is common in government budgets. They show that a resident cannot easily compare one year with the next. That is why this page asks the same questions, with the same definitions, every year.

Source: 2027 Preliminary Operating Budget, pages 8, 9, 27, 28 and 29; Port of Olympia adopted budgets for 2020, 2021, 2024, 2025 and 2026, income statements (links below).

Budget words, translated

The Port's budget book uses these terms. Tap one to see what it means.

Depreciation

Wear and tear. Docks, pavement and buildings lose value every year and will need replacing. Depreciation is the yearly estimate of that loss.

General and administrative (G&A)

Overhead. The shared office costs no single business owns: management, finance, legal, human resources and computers.

Operating and non-operating

Operating is money from running the four businesses. Non-operating is everything else: property taxes, grants, interest, cleanup and loan payments.

Net margin and net income

Net margin is what the businesses have left after running costs and overhead ($37,000). Net income adds taxes and grants and subtracts wear and tear and public costs ($7.2M). They answer different questions.

Tax levy

The total property tax the Port collects in a year. State law lets it grow 1% a year plus tax on new construction, unless voters approve more.

Capital investment plan

The list of things the Port plans to build or buy that last for years, such as a warehouse or a repaved taxiway.

Debt service

Yearly loan payments, including interest.
